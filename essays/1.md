## Old Habits, New Labels

I have always been the kind of programmer who just wants to write the code and move on. Python let me do that. C and C++ let me do that, mostly, as long as I remembered to compile. Arduino and CircuitPython let me do that too. Then TypeScript showed up and wanted a label for everything—every variable, every function, every little piece of data had to announce what it was before it was allowed to do anything. It felt less like writing code and more like filling out name tags at a conference where everyone already knew each other.

None of the underlying ideas were unfamiliar. Variables, functions, loops, arrays, classes—I had used all of these before, in more than one language. What actually slowed me down was translation. I knew *what* I wanted to do; I just kept tripping over *how* TypeScript wanted me to say it. So the real learning curve wasn't programming from scratch. It was more like moving to a new country where you already know the grammar but keep reaching for the wrong word.

`let` and `const` were the first labels that tripped me up. Coming from C, C++, and Python, I was used to variables that didn't ask permission to change:

```typescript
let score = 0;        // this can change later
const maxScore = 100; // this one is locked in, permanently
```

Small syntax, but it forced a small shift in mindset—deciding up front whether something was allowed to change was a new habit, not just a new keyword.

## Sticky Notes That Actually Do Something

Once I got past the initial friction, I started to appreciate what all that labeling was actually for. TypeScript's type system isn't just decoration—it catches mistakes before the program ever runs, instead of letting them hide until something breaks in production. At first that felt restrictive, like being micromanaged by my own code. But the more I sat with it, the more it made sense, especially for anything bigger than a personal script.

That's really the heart of it: code doesn't just belong to the person who wrote it. Other people have to read it, trust it, and eventually change it without breaking everything else. A clear label on a variable or a function is a small kindness to whoever opens that file next—including future me, who will absolutely have forgotten what `data` was supposed to be. I wouldn't say TypeScript is objectively "better" than the other languages I know. But I get why it earns its popularity on larger projects, where structure stops being a nuisance and starts being a safety net.

## Labeling Myself as a "Quiet Room" Coder

The other big shift this module came with a name I'd never heard before: athletic software engineering. Programming as a timed workout, complete with a countdown clock and music playing in the background—that combination was new territory for me, and not exactly comfortable territory.

If I'm being honest, the timer was the hardest part to accept. I like silence. I like having room to think before I touch the keyboard. A ticking clock and a soundtrack are basically the opposite of my ideal working conditions. But the WODs did something useful anyway: after enough of them, I stopped watching the clock and started watching the problem instead. I got better at reading a task, breaking it into pieces, sketching pseudocode, and only then writing real code—which turned out to matter even more under time pressure, because there's no room left to recover from misreading the problem in the first place.

## Speed Was Never Really the Point

Here's the thing I didn't expect: the WODs didn't really make me type faster. They made me *understand* faster—parsing an assignment, sorting out what actually mattered, and organizing my approach before committing to code. That skill doesn't stay inside the classroom. Timed technical interviews are a real part of the software job hunt, and now I've at least practiced sitting in that particular kind of discomfort before it counts for something.

I still wouldn't call timed coding my preferred style. Given more time, I like exploring alternate solutions and digging into the details. But the WODs stretched a muscle I hadn't used much—working well even when the clock isn't on my side.

## The Label That Matters Most

If I had to put a single label on what I learned this module, it wouldn't be "TypeScript" or "WODs" on their own. It would be something closer to *adaptability*. Learning TypeScript wasn't really about learning to program again—it was learning to relabel concepts I already understood. Athletic software engineering wasn't really about speed—it was learning to work clearly even when the conditions weren't the ones I'd choose.

I still prefer quiet rooms and generous deadlines. But I walked out of this module with proof that I can perform outside of that comfort zone, and that's a label I'm willing to keep.
